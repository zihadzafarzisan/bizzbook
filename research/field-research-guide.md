# Bizzbook field research guide

Prepared: 15 November 2026. Status: proposed research; no interviews or outreach performed.

Purpose: learn whether a Facebook-like business network helps Bangladeshi manufacturers, wholesalers, and entrepreneurs make useful connections and return for more business activity. Preserve the all-category vision while comparing concrete commercial situations.

## 1. Recruitment proposal

Start with 30 exploratory interviews: six manufacturer/factory representatives, six wholesalers/distributors, six entrepreneurs who recently made a first stock purchase, six people actively planning a first purchase, and six established small retailers/resellers. These quotas are practical targets, not a representative sample or a statistical power calculation.

Include participants outside the founder’s immediate network, people outside Dhaka, women entrepreneurs, a range of device confidence, and at least a few who prefer offline trade. Avoid treating willingness to talk as willingness to use Bizzbook.

Use several categories to preserve the intended breadth. Record category, location, role, purchase situation, normal order size, sourcing cycle, and current tools. Report small subgroup counts rather than presenting percentages as market estimates.

Suggested recruitment sources: existing supplier/buyer relationships, authorized introductions through associations or training groups, and opt-in notices in communities where admins permit research. Published directories can help identify appropriate organizations. Do not imply those organizations endorse Bizzbook.

No invitation should be sent until outreach is explicitly authorized. No private conversations, identity documents, customer lists, or other people’s contact details are needed to establish the basic opportunity.

## 2. Opening script

“We are researching how people find suppliers, buyers, and useful business contacts in Bangladesh. We want to understand what you do today. This is research, and there is no obligation to use or buy anything. You can skip any question. May I take notes? We will ask separately before recording or retaining examples.”

Explain note storage and retention before collecting identifying information. Redact third-party details from any examples the participant chooses to show. Report patterns without exposing individual businesses’ confidential terms.

## 3. Buyers and entrepreneurs: 25–35 minutes

Begin with the last real event, not a pitch for the app.

1. What business are you running or preparing to start? What have you already done?
2. Tell me about the last time you looked for a product or supplier. When was it, and what triggered the search?
3. Walk me through where you looked, who you contacted, and in what order.
4. What quantity, budget, timing, or location requirements mattered?
5. How did you decide which people or offers were worth a reply?
6. What information was missing? What did you have to ask more than once?
7. What happened after the first conversation: quote, sample, visit, order, or no further action?
8. Why did you proceed or stop? What alternatives did you have?
9. What made you trust or doubt the supplier? Which evidence changed your decision?
10. Where do you keep suppliers, offers, or past discussions so you can find them again?
11. When did you next need a supplier or new product? Was it the same person or someone new?
12. Have you paid for any tool, membership, travel, or service to find supply? What did you get?

For aspiring entrepreneurs, ask what money/time they have already committed and when a real purchase is expected. Avoid interpreting enthusiasm or general browsing as immediate buying demand.

For established retailers, ask when they last changed suppliers, why, and what would have to happen before they considered an unfamiliar one.

## 4. Suppliers: 25–35 minutes

1. Who normally buys from you, and what quantities do they order?
2. Walk me through the last new buyer you acquired. How did the relationship start?
3. Which channels produce enquiries, and which produce buyers who actually fit?
4. What makes an enquiry worth answering? What makes you ignore one?
5. Describe a recent enquiry from a beginner. What happened?
6. What are your minimum quantities, and do they differ for ready stock, samples, or custom production?
7. What product, price, capacity, or customer information are you comfortable publishing? What stays private?
8. Who creates posts and replies to messages? How much effort does it take?
9. How do you communicate stock changes, sold-out items, and new arrivals?
10. How do repeat customers place orders or contact you again?
11. What promotion or business software have you paid for recently? What result justified it?
12. What would prevent you from adding another business channel?

Where relevant, ask whether the representative can approve offers and whether the stock can be sold to the intended local buyers. Record uncertainty; do not assume a directory listing settles either question.

## 5. Short Bangla prompts

Use the participant’s preferred language and everyday business terms. These are original interview prompts, not translations from a study.

- শেষবার নতুন সাপ্লায়ার কীভাবে খুঁজেছিলেন?
- প্রথমে কোথায় খুঁজেছেন, তারপর কাকে যোগাযোগ করেছেন?
- কত পিস দরকার ছিল, আর কত দিনের মধ্যে দরকার ছিল?
- সাপ্লায়ারকে বিশ্বাস করার আগে কী কী দেখেছেন?
- কথা বলার পর কী হলো—দাম জানলেন, স্যাম্পল নিলেন, নাকি অর্ডার করলেন?
- অর্ডার না করলে, কেন করেননি?
- পরেরবার দরকার হলে আগের সাপ্লায়ারের কাছে গিয়েছিলেন, নাকি নতুন কাউকে খুঁজেছিলেন?
- কোন ধরনের ইনকোয়ারির উত্তর দিতে আপনার সুবিধা হয়?
- কোন তথ্য পোস্টে দিতে চান, আর কোনটা ব্যক্তিগতভাবে বলতে চান?
- নতুন কোনো অ্যাপ ব্যবহার করতে হলে আপনার কী সুবিধা দরকার?

Follow a general answer with “শেষবার এমন কখন হয়েছিল?” to return to a concrete example.

## 6. Concept test after the current workflow

Describe Bizzbook neutrally: “An app where people publish business posts, discover products and businesses in a feed, follow one another, and send DMs.”

Show candidate post types and tasks only after documenting current behaviour. Use fictional businesses clearly marked as examples. Do not imply that suppliers, stock, verification, or guarantees exist.

Suggested tasks:

- Find a supplier for the requirement discussed earlier.
- Decide which of two offers deserves a conversation and explain why.
- Start an enquiry with the information the supplier needs.
- Find a previously saved offer or followed supplier.
- Publish a buyer request or a seller update.
- Explain what a proposed verification label means.

Observe actions, hesitation, missing information, and misunderstandings. Ask what they would still do in Facebook, WhatsApp, by phone, or in person. Avoid “Would you use our app?” and “Would you like verified suppliers?” as primary evidence.

## 7. Pilot protocol

After interviews, run a small pilot with real consenting participants and genuine offers/requirements. The feed and DMs can be simple; do not add payment or protection promises just for the experiment.

Use 20–30 suppliers and 60–100 prospective buyers as initial planning ranges, adjusted to recruitment access and actual requirements. Interview quotas and pilot sizes need not match. These numbers are not market-size estimates or sufficient to prove national demand.

Record who recruited each participant, incentives, staff onboarding time, staff prompts, manually arranged introductions, and whether each action was voluntary or assisted. Users should know when staff are involved. Do not manufacture buyer requests or supplier replies to make the network appear active.

Check a sample of business outcomes with consent. Keep categories separate: participant-reported quote, buyer-confirmed suitable offer, sample requested, reported purchase, and purchase corroborated by both sides or permitted evidence. No purchase should be called verified simply because a chat occurred.

## 8. Measurement definitions

| Measure | Definition | Interpretation limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyer activation | Share of recruited buyers who complete a predefined genuine sourcing/discovery task within seven days | Define the task before recruitment; report assisted activation separately |
| Requirement coverage | Requirements receiving a compatible supplier response within the observation window / genuine requirements posted | Specify response window and compatibility criteria in advance |
| Qualified conversation | Both parties respond about a specific real offer or requirement | Does not prove purchase, satisfaction, or retention |
| Time to usable response | Time from a genuine enquiry/request to the first buyer-confirmed applicable answer | Unanswered requests must remain visible in reporting |
| Four-week buyer retention | Members of an eligible recruited-buyer cohort performing a predefined meaningful activity in days 22–28 / that cohort | Keep non-activated recruits in the primary cohort denominator; report activated-cohort retention separately |
| Supplier retention | Same cohort method, using useful supplier activity such as responding or refreshing a genuine offer | Repeated duplicate posting is not evidence of useful participation |
| Cycle-based return | Return at the participant’s next observed sourcing/production cycle | Needed where a weekly cadence is unsuitable |
| Useful outcome | Predefined decision progress or result, with evidence level recorded | Separate self-report, counterpart confirmation, and direct observation |
| Staff effort | Recruitment, onboarding, prompting, matching, and support time | Necessary to distinguish scalable behaviour from staff-created activity |
| Paid conversion and renewal | Actual payments for a real optional service, followed through the next eligible renewal | Verbal intent or a price-survey answer is not a paid customer |

“Meaningful activity” should advance a real business purpose: a relevant enquiry, a supplier response, a request update, or revisiting a saved offer for a current task. Define the event set before evaluating results. Count unique people/businesses and pairs; do not inflate the result with repeated messages.

Report cohort counts by purchasing situation, category, and recruitment method. Note incomplete follow-up and users not yet eligible for a four-week window. Do not compare a day-10 cohort with one observed for six weeks.

## 9. Decision rules to agree before the pilot

Choose numeric thresholds after estimating recruitment access, baseline workflows, and the cost of running the pilot. There is no sourced universal retention threshold for Bizzbook.

Continue investigating when independent buyers repeatedly obtain suitable responses and return for business reasons, suppliers participate with decreasing help, and acquisition/support effort appears manageable.

Rework the proposition when the feed is active but requirements remain unanswered, beginners cannot meet available quantities, participants cannot interpret trust signals, or useful activity depends on constant staff intervention.

If people leave, ask whether they succeeded and now trade directly, failed to find a fit, stopped doing business, forgot the app, or preferred another tool. Those outcomes call for different decisions.

## 10. Analysis record

For each interview or pilot case, capture: anonymous ID, date, participant situation, category/location, recent task, existing workflow, constraint, evidence/example with consent, result, alternative explanation, and follow-up question.

Maintain four separate labels: **observed**, **participant-reported**, **researcher interpretation**, and **hypothesis**. Record contradictory cases alongside supportive ones. Interview counts show coverage of the exploratory sample, not the percentage of Bangladesh businesses with a problem.
